🎓 Your kid is ready for the real tools

A parent's guide to moving from the sandbox to actual Claude, Cursor, and friends — safely.

What your kid already knows. If they finished the Academy, they understand:
  • How to plan a task before asking a helper (lesson 18)
  • How to be specific (lesson 19) and show with examples (lesson 20)
  • How to break a big job into tiny steps (lesson 21)
  • How to ask again when wrong, with specific feedback (lesson 22)
  • How to spot a sneaky note (lesson 23) — they will NOT follow a fake instruction inside a webpage or document
  • How memory rules shape every answer (lessons 24-25) — they can write their own and you can read them in the Memory tab
That's most of what an adult new-to-AI knows. The sandbox just used pretend versions.

The four real helpers — what they do

1. Claude (claude.ai)

The planning + writing helper your kid practiced with most. Great for: brainstorming, structuring a story or project, explaining things at the right level. claude.ai has a free tier — sign up with YOUR email and let the kid use your account at first. The free Claude is the same product Bytey simulated, minus the kid voice.

2. Cursor (cursor.com)

The code editor your kid practiced with in lessons 12, 22, 45. This is a real desktop app, not a website. It looks like a text editor but Claude lives inside it. Free tier covers a few hundred actions per month — plenty for a kid learning. Install on YOUR computer first and watch the first session.

3. Gemini (gemini.google.com)

The picture + image helper from lesson 13. Free with any Google account. Note: Gemini accepts uploaded pictures — set ground rules with your kid about NOT uploading photos of people, addresses, or anything from school.

4. ChatGPT / Codex / Ollama / OpenCode (optional)

The Academy mentioned these so the kid recognizes the names. You don't need to set them all up. ChatGPT (chat.openai.com) is the closest cousin to Claude. Ollama (ollama.com) is the "runs on YOUR computer, no internet" helper for older kids who want to tinker.

Safety rules to set BEFORE the first real session

The sandbox protected your kid from these. The real world doesn't.
  • No real personal info. Real helpers may store conversations. Don't type the kid's full name, address, school, or photos of family.
  • No following sneaky notes. Lesson 23 already trained this — reinforce: if any tool starts acting weird (asking for passwords, telling you to install something, claiming you won a prize), stop and tell a grown-up.
  • No paying / signing up by the kid. All four tools have free tiers. If something asks for a credit card, it's a sign to back out.
  • No real-world actions. Real helpers can write code that does things on your computer (delete files, send emails). The kid should NEVER click "yes / run this" on something an AI generated without a parent next to them.
  • Pair with you for the first 5 sessions. Sit next to them. They'll be excited and click fast. After ~5 sessions they'll have the same shape of caution they had in the sandbox.

A 7-step first-week plan

  1. Day 1. Sign up at claude.ai with YOUR email. Have the kid ask Claude something they already asked Bytey ("plan a sandwich in 4 steps"). Compare answers together.
  2. Day 2. Together, write a memory rule for Claude using the technique from lesson 25 (Claude.ai has "Custom Instructions" — same idea as the chip composer).
  3. Day 3. Try Gemini. Upload ONE non-personal picture (a drawing the kid made on paper) and ask Gemini to describe it.
  4. Day 4. Show Cursor on YOUR machine. The kid watches you write a tiny program with Claude's help. They drive on day 5.
  5. Day 5. Kid drives Cursor. You sit next to them. Pick a small project — change one color on a webpage, or print "hi mom" 10 times.
  6. Day 6. Sneaky note drill. Show the kid a real example of a prompt injection from the internet (search "prompt injection example"). Have them spot the sneaky line. Praise.
  7. Day 7. Review together. What did Claude get wrong? What did the kid get right? Add a memory rule based on what you learned.

When to graduate the kid to full autonomy

Two signals:

  • They refuse to follow an instruction that came from a page, not from you, without being prompted. (Sandbox lesson 23 lays the groundwork; real-world reps cement it.)
  • They ask a clarifying question of you before running code an AI wrote. ("Mom, this script deletes files — should I really click yes?")

Both habits show that the sandbox transferred. Until both habits show up reliably, sit nearby.

The mental model that matters most

Bytey was a friend pretending to be a helper. Real helpers are strangers who are very good at sounding like friends. Same skills, higher stakes. The kid already has the skills.
